<h2 class="lecture-chapter">Chapter 1 — Reading States Through Time</h2>

<section class="lecture-slide" id="slide-1">
  <div class="slide-copy">
    <h2>From Brain Whisper to Human Experience</h2>
    <p>This page presents the ideas I shared in a 2026 omnibus lecture for the “Frontiers of Business” course at Fukuoka International College of Music. I spoke about the place of music in my life, my work as a physician and medical researcher, and the vision I hope to pursue.</p>
    <p>This is not simply a story about a doctor who continued to play music. What I want to explore is how small changes accumulate into larger trajectories and ultimately become human experience.</p>
    <p>In medicine, we follow a patient’s symptoms and clinical course. In research, we observe changes in neurons and brain states. In music, each note acquires meaning through time.</p>
    <p>By connecting these perspectives, I would like to consider a single theme: <em>From Brain Whisper to Human Experience</em>.</p>
    <p>At several points, I use clinical examples. These are based on experiences I have encountered directly or learned about through others, but details have been modified to protect patient privacy.</p>

<section class="lecture-slide" id="slide-2">

    <h2>A State Is a Trace of Transition</h2>
    <p>This is Marcel Duchamp’s <em>Nude Descending a Staircase</em>.</p>
    <p>The human body is not represented as a single, frozen figure. Instead, successive moments of a body descending the stairs—the process of changing through time—are layered into one image.</p>
    <p>I have always been drawn to this painting because it resembles what I want to see in medicine, research, and music.</p>
    <p>We often describe things as if they were still images: “This person is now in this condition,” “The brain is now in this state,” or “This note is C.” Yet every state contains the changes that led to it.</p>
    <p>In medicine, the same symptom can mean something different depending on the path by which it emerged. In research, brain states such as sleep and wakefulness arise from neural firing and ionic changes accumulating over time. In music, the same note can carry a completely different meaning depending on the harmony and phrase that precede it and where the music is moving next.</p>
    <p>A state, then, is not merely something that exists at one moment. It is a trace of transition. Using this perspective, I would like to ask how small changes in neurons become brain states, music, and ultimately human experience.</p>

<section class="lecture-slide" id="slide-3">

    <h2>How Small Changes Become Experience</h2>
    <p>If a state is a trace of transition, where does that transition begin? For me, its smallest units were neuronal firing and the movement of ions such as Na⁺.</p>
    <p>Each spike is a tiny event. Repeated through time, however, spikes form patterns, and those patterns become brain states. Brain states, in turn, shape human experiences such as sleepiness, concentration, emotion, and consciousness.</p>
    <p>Music follows a similar logic. A single sound is not yet music. When sounds unfold through time, enter into relationships, and create tension and resolution, they become music—and music becomes human experience.</p>
    <p>This lecture follows that sequence as a map: from Neuron to Brain, from Brain to Music, and from Music to Human Experience. I will also use this map to reflect on my own life.</p>

    <h2>Listening Is a Fundamental Way of Understanding the World</h2>
    <p>I would like to begin with one keyword: listening.</p>
    <p>At a music college, “listening” naturally brings sound to mind. For me, however, listening is not limited to music.</p>
    <p>In medicine, we listen to patients. This means more than hearing a list of complaints. We ask when the symptoms began, how they changed, and what made them better or worse. We listen for the flow of time behind the symptoms.</p>
    <p>In research, we listen to data—to neuronal firing, EEG signals, and the behavior of models. We look for patterns, for the points at which a state changes, and for the variables that create that transition.</p>
    <p>In music, we listen to each note, but never in isolation. We hear the harmony that came before it, where it is moving next, and the meaning it acquires within time.</p>
    <p>Medicine, research, and music are therefore all practices of listening for change through time. Throughout this lecture, I will move among these three forms of listening.</p>

    <p>This lecture is not simply the story of someone who became a doctor and continued playing music. Music has certainly been a major presence in my life, but I do not want to describe it merely as a hobby that persisted alongside medicine.</p>

<h2 class="lecture-chapter">Chapter 2 — Brain Whisper: What Medicine and Research Have Shown Me</h2>

<section class="lecture-slide" id="slide-6">
  <div class="slide-copy">
    <h2>Medicine, Research, and Music as Parallel Streams of Time</h2>
    <p>Let me briefly introduce myself. I graduated from the Faculty of Medicine at the University of Tokyo and now work as a physician at Kurume University Hospital while studying sleep and neural activity in the University of Tokyo’s graduate program. My research asks how microscopic phenomena—brain activity and the movement of small particles such as sodium and calcium—give rise to brain states such as sleep and wakefulness. At the same time, I have played the piano since childhood, with a particular focus on Chopin. Today I would like to describe how medicine, research, and music have become connected within me.</p>

<section class="lecture-slide" id="slide-7">
  <div class="slide-copy">
    <h2>The Multiple Levels of Medicine and Research</h2>
    <p>One reason I was drawn to medicine is that a human being cannot be explained at only one level. We are molecules, cells, organs, minds, and social beings. In clinical medicine, all of these levels appear simultaneously within a single patient.</p>

  <figure class="slide-image">

    <h2>The Diversity of Clinical Trajectories</h2>
    <p>Patients do not enter the clinic exactly as textbooks describe them. Symptoms arise not only from disease itself, but within the contexts of daily life, sleep, work, family, emotion, and the social environment. Even with the same diagnosis, each patient follows a different course. The same medication may work well for one person and less well for another. Many of these differences remain questions for future medical research, but if we want to choose the best possible treatment for each patient, we must attend closely to the particular pattern of that patient’s condition.</p>
  </div>
  <figure class="slide-image">
    <img src="/assets/img/8a_en.jpeg" alt="The Diversity of Clinical Trajectories">

<section class="lecture-slide" id="slide-9">
  <div class="slide-copy">
    <h2>Long Transitions: A Biological Timeline Beyond the Diagnosis</h2>
    <p>Let me offer one concrete example: breast cancer, which affects approximately one in nine women in Japan over a lifetime. Advances in treatment have made long-term survival increasingly possible, yet breast cancer can sometimes recur after a long interval. To protect patient privacy, I have modified some details. Imagine a patient who underwent surgery and systemic therapy and was then followed without evidence of recurrence. Twenty-five years after treatment, however, the cancer recurred.</p>
    <p>For both the patient and the clinicians, many years may pass with the feeling that “treatment is over” or “perhaps everything is all right now.” A recurrence 25 years later can therefore seem like a sudden event. Medically, however, those 25 years are not simply an empty interval. In breast cancer, a very small number of residual cancer cells may persist for a long time in a dormant state, with their proliferation suppressed and their presence below the resolution of clinical imaging. If that equilibrium is later disrupted, those cells may resume growth and eventually become detectable as recurrent disease.</p>
    <p>For any individual patient, however, it is rarely possible to identify exactly what maintained dormancy or what triggered renewed growth. The long-term course must be considered in the context of changes across the whole body, including immunity, the local tissue environment, aging, inflammation, and metabolism.</p>
    <p>The important point is that recurrence cannot be understood solely as an event occurring “right now.” The present condition bears traces of the initial diagnosis and treatment, the life lived afterward, and the long transitions that unfolded within the body.</p>
    <p>Clinical medicine therefore requires us to see more than a diagnostic label. We must listen for the patient’s background, the treatments received, the time that has passed, and the path by which the present state emerged.</p>
    <p>This is one of the most important sensibilities medicine has taught me: medicine is the work of listening to change through time.</p>
  </div>
  <figure class="slide-image">
    <img src="/assets/img/9a_en.jpeg" alt="Long Transitions: A Biological Timeline Beyond the Diagnosis">
  </figure>
</section>

<section class="lecture-slide" id="slide-10">
  <div class="slide-copy">
    <h2>To Understand Time Is to Understand the Human Being</h2>
    <p>The example of late breast-cancer recurrence involved an interval of 25 years. Yet medicine is not concerned only with long spans of time.</p>
    <p>Neurons fire on the scale of milliseconds. Heart rate and breathing fluctuate over seconds. Drug effects, metabolism, and symptoms may change over minutes or hours. Sleep and wakefulness alternate within a daily rhythm. Meanwhile, chronic disease progression, responses to treatment, cancer recurrence, aging, and life itself unfold over years and decades.</p>
    <p>Medicine is therefore an effort to understand human beings across many different timescales, from the extremely brief to the extraordinarily long.</p>
    <p>This is also one reason I was drawn to medicine and research. A person cannot be understood by examining a single instant. Neuronal time, organ and metabolic time, sleep and circadian time, the time course of illness, and the time of a life all overlap to produce one human state.</p>
    <p>This sensibility is remarkably close to music. Music contains the short time of an individual note, the time of a phrase, the time of a movement and an entire work, and the time that the music occupies within a person’s life. Both medicine and music engage with things that change through time.</p>
    <p>Coming from the world of music, I became increasingly fascinated by time, rhythm, oscillation, and state transitions. That interest naturally led me to chronobiology and to the study of sleep and wakefulness.</p>
    <p>Let us now turn to the phenomenon of sleep–wake regulation that forms the core of my research.</p>
  </div>
  <figure class="slide-image">
    <img src="/assets/img/10a_en.jpeg" alt="To Understand Time Is to Understand the Human Being">
  </figure>
</section>

<section class="lecture-slide" id="slide-11">
  <div class="slide-copy">
    <h2>Dynamic Switching of Brain States: Sleep and Wakefulness</h2>
    <p>From this perspective, sleep and wakefulness became a natural focus for my research.</p>
    <p>Every day we wake, sleep, and wake again. Because this cycle is so familiar, sleep and wakefulness can seem almost self-evident.</p>
    <p>As a scientific phenomenon, however, the cycle is remarkably deep. Falling asleep is not merely becoming less alert or closing one’s eyes. It is a major change in the mode of activity across the entire brain.</p>
    <p>As shown on the left, we repeatedly move among wakefulness, non-rapid eye movement (NREM) sleep, and rapid eye movement (REM) sleep. These are fundamental brain-state transitions that occur many times over the course of a night.</p>
    <p>The center panel represents the electroencephalogram, or EEG. Wakefulness is characterized by relatively low-amplitude, high-frequency activity. During NREM sleep, high-amplitude, low-frequency slow-wave activity becomes prominent. What appears from the outside to be a single state called “sleep” therefore contains profound changes in the brain’s activity pattern.</p>
    <p>As shown on the right, cortical neurons during NREM sleep alternate between active up states and quieter down states. During an up state, neurons are depolarized and more likely to fire. During a down state, they become hyperpolarized and activity subsides. This alternation is known as the up–down oscillation.</p>
    <p>The key point is that the familiar transition between sleep and wakefulness is supported by changes across multiple levels: neuronal firing, EEG activity, ionic movement, and network state.</p>
    <p>Sleep and wakefulness are not merely features of a lifestyle schedule. They are states of the brain that switch through time.</p>
    <p>What small changes generate those switches? The next section introduces the Na⁺ dynamics—the movement of sodium within neurons—that became central to my research.</p>
  </div>
  <figure class="slide-image">
    <img src="/assets/img/11a_en.jpeg" alt="Dynamic Switching of Brain States: Sleep and Wakefulness">
  </figure>
</section>

<section class="lecture-slide" id="slide-12">
  <div class="slide-copy">
    <h2>How the State of a Protein Helps Shape Consciousness</h2>
    <p>Here I would like to briefly introduce the central idea behind my research.</p>
    <p>Na⁺ channels and Na⁺ ions are essential to neurons. When Na⁺ channels open, Na⁺ enters the cell, changing the membrane potential and generating neuronal firing—an action potential. In that sense, Na⁺ is one of the most fundamental drivers of neuronal activation.</p>
    <p>But Na⁺ does more than excite the cell. When firing continues, Na⁺ gradually accumulates within the neuron. That accumulated Na⁺ activates pathways such as Na⁺-dependent K⁺ channels, or KNa channels, and the Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase. These mechanisms hyperpolarize the cell and push it toward a quieter state.</p>
    <p>Na⁺ therefore has a dual role. It activates the neuron, yet as it accumulates it also engages the machinery that silences the cell. It acts as an accelerator while also switching on the brake.</p>
    <p>The properties of the Na⁺ channel itself are equally important. A channel is not simply open or closed. The voltage at which it opens, how readily it closes, how slowly it inactivates, and how it recovers can all differ. Small differences in these properties alter the firing rhythm of a neuron.</p>
    <p>When the rhythms of individual neurons change, the way in which they synchronize also changes. This resembles an orchestra: a subtle change in each player’s tempo, entrance, or note length can transform the resonance and cohesion of the ensemble. In the brain, changes in the rhythms of individual cells likewise alter the collective activity pattern.</p>
    <p>Changes in whole-brain rhythm and synchrony then contribute to macroscopic human experiences such as sleep and wakefulness, drowsiness, concentration, emotion, and behavior.</p>
    <p>This is what “Brain Whisper” means to me. The states of tiny components—Na⁺ channels and Na⁺ ions—alter neuronal rhythm, reshape synchrony across the brain, and ultimately influence human experience. A small molecular state may help tune a large biological phenomenon.</p>
  </div>
  <figure class="slide-image">
    <img src="/assets/img/12a_en.jpeg" alt="How the State of a Protein Helps Shape Consciousness">
  </figure>
</section>

<section class="lecture-slide" id="slide-13">
  <div class="slide-copy">
    <h2>Small Molecular States Become Rhythms and Shape Complexity Through Time</h2>
    <p>Let me summarize one idea that has emerged from my research.</p>
    <p>We wake, sleep, and wake again every day. Behind this familiar cycle, neuronal firing, Na⁺ flow, cellular excitability, and rhythm are changing continuously.</p>
    <p>Each spike is small. Each influx of Na⁺ is small. Yet as these events accumulate and form patterns over time, the state of the entire brain changes.</p>
    <p>What fascinates me is that biological complexity may not always require countless independent mechanisms operating separately.</p>
    <p>Instead, subtle differences among the states available to a small number of molecules and channels—how readily they open or close, how quickly they inactivate, and how they recover—may generate diverse temporal patterns. The combination of those patterns may then produce brain rhythms and state transitions.</p>
    <p>The phrase at the bottom of the slide, “Biological complexity from a few molecular states,” expresses precisely this idea.</p>
    <p>The complexity of life may arise not merely from having many components, but from the possible states of a few components, the ways those states change, and how they combine through time.</p>
    <p>This is one of the broad ideas I have developed through research on sleep and wakefulness.</p>
    <p>It is also surprisingly close to music. Music does not become rich simply by accumulating countless sounds. A few notes, short motifs, and changes in rhythm or harmony can combine through time to create an extraordinarily rich musical experience.</p>
    <p>We will now consider how this view—that small changes acquire meaning through time—has connected research with music in my own life.</p>
  </div>

<h2 class="lecture-chapter">Chapter 3 — Music as Time: A Note Gains Meaning in Context</h2>

<section class="lecture-slide" id="slide-14">
  <div class="slide-copy">
    <h2>Music Has Continued as Another Stream of Time</h2>
    <p>Let me briefly describe my relationship with the piano. I began playing in 2004. Through performances and competitions during my school years, I learned how to express, listen, and practice. I later entered medicine and research and now work as a physician, but music did not disappear along the way. It has continued in changing forms, including performances in hospitals and charity concerts. Music was never a separate life; it was another stream of time running alongside medicine and research.</p>
    <p>So far, I have described how the states of individual proteins can create cellular rhythms and how those rhythms can shape sleep, wakefulness, and behavior across the brain and body. I would now like to ask a parallel question in music: how does the flow of individual notes create a larger temporal arc? Because I have focused especially on Chopin, I will use two of his works as examples.</p>
  </div>
  <figure class="slide-image">
    <img src="/assets/img/14a_en.jpeg" alt="Music Has Continued as Another Stream of Time">
  </figure>
</section>

<section class="lecture-slide" id="slide-15">
  <div class="slide-copy">
    <h2>Small Shifts in Time Create a Rotational Sense of the Body</h2>
    <p>Let us begin with Chopin’s Mazurka in C-sharp minor, Op. 59 No. 3.</p>
    <p>The term “mazurka” does not refer to one single dance. It is a broad name for Polish folk dances in triple meter. These include the slower, more song-like <em>kujawiak</em>, the moderately paced and strongly accented <em>mazur</em>, and the faster, more rotational <em>oberek</em>.</p>
    <p>Op. 59 No. 3 is marked <em>Vivace</em>. It can be heard as a mazurka grounded in the <em>mazur</em> but interwoven at points with the character of the <em>oberek</em>. It is not merely an elegant triple-meter dance; it contains rapid rotation, leaps, and shifts in bodily weight.</p>
    <p>Consider the opening. Although the meter is 3/4, the music should not be divided into mechanically equal beats of one, two, three. Accents on the third beat, slurs, support from the left hand, and subtle agogic accents shift the center of gravity.</p>
    <p>Rhythm is therefore not a perfectly uniform clock. Musical time emerges through accent, delay, weight, rotation, and bodily movement.</p>
    <p>Now consider the <em>oberek</em>-like passage beginning at measure 71. The meter remains triple, yet the character changes. Longer phrases move lightly forward without the earlier emphasis on the third beat, creating a completely different bodily sensation.</p>
    <p>This is more than the appearance of a new melody. It feels as though the body has entered another state within the same piece.</p>
    <p>The point is that, in a mazurka, small temporal shifts create meaning. A momentary wait, a subtle transfer of weight, or a change in the unit of the dance can transform the felt speed of the music.</p>
    <p>This resembles the way I think about brain rhythms. Even when two patterns appear to have the same period, the collective activity changes depending on where elements align, where they diverge, and where the center of gravity falls. The sleep-related neural activity I showed earlier can acquire entirely different properties and rhythms through very small shifts. Op. 59 No. 3 teaches us how microtiming creates embodied meaning.</p>
  </div>
  <figure class="slide-image">
    <img src="/assets/img/15a_en.jpeg" alt="Small Shifts in Time Create a Rotational Sense of the Body">
  </figure>
</section>

<section class="lecture-slide" id="slide-16">
  <div class="slide-copy">
    <h2>The Same Theme Changes Meaning When Its History Changes</h2>
    <p>Next, let us consider Chopin’s Ballade No. 3 in A-flat major, Op. 47.</p>
    <p>The mazurka illustrated microtime: meaning created by small shifts in timing. In the Third Ballade, by contrast, the meaning of a theme changes across a much longer temporal span.</p>
    <p>The work opens with an introduction in A-flat major. Rather than treating it immediately as the story’s first theme, I hear it as an entrance into the story—a narrative threshold that creates the space in which the music can begin to speak.</p>
    <p>The first theme then appears in C major. It begins gently, almost as a <em>mezza voce</em> narration. Rather than making a forceful statement, the music quietly opens the story. At this stage, the theme appears calm and composed.</p>
    <p>When the second theme arrives in A-flat major, the state of the music changes. It becomes more animated; the harmonic direction shifts, and another kind of time begins to flow within the same work.</p>
    <p>The first theme later returns, but after the influence of the development it now appears in A-flat major rather than C major. Themes return several times across the piece, yet a returning theme never means exactly what it meant at first. The listener has already passed through other developmental passages, harmonic shifts, and trajectories toward different climaxes.</p>
    <p>For example, the returning theme inherits the energy of the development and feels more alive than its quiet, almost innocent first appearance.</p>
    <p>I began by saying that a state is a trace of transition. In music, even when the same theme returns, the time through which we have traveled changes the experience of hearing it.</p>
    <p>The same is true in medicine. “Breast cancer has been detected” does not mean the same thing in an initial presentation as it does after surgery, radiotherapy, systemic therapy, and years of subsequent life. The history changes the meaning of the present state.</p>
    <p>The Third Ballade shows how a long temporal history can transform the meaning of what appears to be the same material.</p>

    <h2>Sensibilities Cultivated Through Music</h2>

Studying a score taught me to read the structure behind the notation. Performing in concerts and listening to many different musicians taught me to hear subtle shifts in timing. I learned to feel weight, breath, tension, and resolution through the body; to develop an interpretation; and to communicate that interpretation to others through sound.</p>
    <p>Music therefore contains analysis, embodiment, hypothesis, revision, and communication. When these processes are integrated through the mind and sensibility of an individual performer, they become art.</p>
    <p>Technically flawless playback can now be automated. We still attend concerts performed by human beings because we want to experience the performer’s interpretation—their particular way of shaping time.</p>

    <p>The same is true of a patient’s words. Behind the presenting complaint lie a clinical course, a life, a body, emotions, and responses to treatment.</p>
    <p>Interpreting a Chopin phrase, reading brain-activity data, and listening to a patient are all, for me, practices of reading the temporal structure beneath the surface.</p>

    <p>We form a hypothesis, turn it into sound, listen to the recording, and revise the interpretation if it did not communicate what we intended.</p>
    <p>Research follows a similar process: observe a phenomenon, formulate a hypothesis, build a model, compare the result with reality, and revise.</p>
    <p>The interpretive process I learned in music has proved remarkably close to the way I think in medical research.</p>

<h2 class="lecture-chapter">Chapter 4 — Expanding What It Means to Build a Life with Music</h2>

<section class="lecture-slide" id="slide-21">
  <div class="slide-copy">
    <h2>Expanding What It Means to Build a Life with Music</h2>
    <p>So far, I have argued that musical practice, despite appearing very different from my present work, shares something fundamental with it. But music itself can also be brought directly into one’s work.</p>
    <p>When we hear the phrase “making a living through music,” it is natural to imagine becoming a professional performer. That is an important path. Music is deep enough to demand an entire lifetime, and if medicine had not drawn me so strongly, I might have spent my days as a musician continually refining my interpretations. Yet performance is not the only destination available to music.</p>
    <p>Society also needs people who deliver music, design musical experiences, apply music, and use music to connect people and communities. The future will require not only those who perform music, but also those who connect music with society.</p>
    <p>The value of music does not exist only on the stage. How can we transform music into an experience within society? I would like to begin answering that question in the field I know best: medicine and healthcare.</p>

<h2 class="lecture-chapter">Chapter 5 — Music × Medicine</h2>

<section class="lecture-slide" id="slide-22">
  <div class="slide-copy">
    <h2>Music × Medicine</h2>
    <p>Music and medicine intersect in important ways because music is deeply connected with human emotion, memory, the body, and rhythm.</p>
    <p>In sleep medicine, psychiatry, dementia care, rehabilitation, palliative care, and well-being, music can be more than entertainment. It can become a way of engaging with a person’s state.</p>
    <p>Music is also a method of understanding human beings—one that reaches the brain, body, emotions, and memory. This creates a natural connection with medicine through music therapy. In music therapy, the creative process can be as important as listening: patients may actively make music with instruments such as castanets or the piano.</p>
  </div>
  <figure class="slide-image">
    <img src="/assets/img/22a_en.jpeg" alt="Music and Medicine">
  </figure>
</section>
<section class="lecture-slide" id="slide-23">
  <div class="slide-copy">
    <h2>Music May Help the Body Return to a State in Which It Can Move</h2>
    <p>As one example of the intersection between music and medicine, I would like to consider functional neurological disorder, or FND.</p>
    <p>The following is a hypothetical case.</p>
    <p>Imagine a woman who has experienced domestic violence over a long period. Persistent fear and tension keep her body in a state of constant vigilance.</p>
    <p>Her heart rate rises. Her muscles tighten. Her breathing becomes shallow. She becomes highly sensitive to unusual sensations in her body.</p>
    <p>These responses are not inherently abnormal. They are part of the body’s natural preparation for danger—the fight-or-flight response. The problem arises when that state persists.</p>
    <p>After prolonged stress or trauma, attention can become increasingly drawn to small bodily reactions: a slight tremor in the hand, a momentary difficulty generating force, or a sense that the leg feels heavy. Signals that would normally remain in the background may be interpreted as warnings that something is dangerous or wrong.</p>
    <p>It is important, however, not to imply that stress or trauma inevitably causes FND. Stress, interpersonal difficulties, injury, physical illness, pain, and fatigue may all be relevant in some patients, but they are neither universal nor required for diagnosis. Even when psychological factors are present, we must be careful not to assume whether they are a direct cause, a predisposing background, or simply a coexisting condition.</p>
    <p>How, then, might motor symptoms such as tremor or weakness arise after severe stress or trauma?</p>
    <p>One key concept is a mismatch between prediction and sensory input.</p>
    <p>The brain does not passively receive information from the body. It continually predicts how the body should move next and what each sensation is likely to mean.</p>
    <p>When we intend to move a hand, for example, the brain does more than issue a motor command. It also predicts how the hand should move, what feedback should return from the joints, what the movement should look like, and what it should feel like to be the person initiating it.</p>
    <p>The brain then compares those predictions with sensory information returning from the body—proprioception, vision, touch, and signals from muscles and joints.</p>
    <p>Normally, prediction and sensory input align sufficiently well that we experience, “I am moving this hand.” This experience is called the sense of agency.</p>
    <p>One model of FND proposes that the weighting of prediction and sensory input becomes disturbed. In functional motor symptoms, feedforward predictions shaped by expectation, past experience, attention, and emotion may become too influential relative to the sensory feedback that actually returns from the body. The mismatch may weaken the person’s sense of agency.</p>
    <p>Put another way, top-down prediction may outweigh bottom-up sensory input.</p>
    <p>“This hand may begin to shake.” “This leg may not move.” “My body may be in danger.”</p>
    <p>When such predictions become overly strong, the brain may interpret and organize bodily activity in a way that conforms to them.</p>
    <p>We can also explain this through the idea of a “map of movement.”</p>
    <p>The brain contains maps that support movement: which muscles should be activated and when, how each joint should move, what sensations should return afterward, and where each part of the body is located. Together, these form a dynamic sensorimotor map.</p>
    <p>This map is not fixed like a printed atlas. The brain updates it continuously by comparing prediction with incoming sensation.</p>
    <p>The same process occurs when playing the piano. We see the keyboard, move a finger, feel the keystroke, and hear the resulting sound. By comparing the predicted sound with the sound that actually occurs, we adjust the next movement. The sensorimotor map is continually relearned through movement and feedback.</p>
    <p>FND can be understood, in part, as a disturbance in access to this sensorimotor map or motor program. The neural pathways are not necessarily structurally destroyed. Rather, the prediction that “this hand can move normally” or “this leg can support me” may weaken, while predictions such as “it will not move” or “it will tremble” become stronger.</p>
    <p>As a result, deliberate attempts to move can draw more attention toward the symptom and make movement feel increasingly awkward.</p>
    <p>In functional tremor, the tremor may become stronger when attention is directed toward it and weaker when attention is diverted. Its frequency and amplitude may vary. If the other hand taps a different rhythm, the tremor may shift toward that rhythm. This phenomenon is called entrainment and, together with distractibility and variability, is a positive clinical sign of functional tremor.</p>
    <p>These signs show that the tremor is not simply the fixed output of structural nerve damage. It is a motor pattern that changes with attention, prediction, and the context of motor control.</p>
    <p>A similar principle can be observed in functional limb weakness.</p>
    <p>The patient genuinely experiences an inability to generate force. Yet with signs such as Hoover’s sign, activating the opposite leg can restore automatic force in the leg that appeared weak. Voluntary movement is difficult, while components of automatic movement remain accessible. This is why explanations of FND often emphasize that the brain’s message does not pass effectively during deliberate movement even though automatic movement can be preserved.</p>
    <p>This is not a story of a damaged muscle or a severed nerve. More precisely, the networks governing access to voluntary movement, bodily prediction, sensory feedback, and agency have entered a maladaptive state.</p>
    <p>Several forms of physiotherapy have already been developed for FND. Based on my own experience with music, I believe the piano may also offer a useful framework to explore—within appropriate multidisciplinary care.</p>
    <p>When a person plays the piano, attention changes. Rather than monitoring whether the hand is shaking or whether enough force is being generated, attention shifts outward toward sound, rhythm, the keyboard, the phrase, and the relationship between the two hands.</p>
    <p>This resembles a central principle of FND rehabilitation: redirecting attention away from the symptom itself. In physiotherapy for functional motor symptoms, excessive attention to the affected body part can worsen performance, whereas diverted attention and more automatic movement may help normal movement re-emerge.</p>
    <p>The piano also provides rhythm.</p>
    <p>Rhythm gives the body an external structure. Trying internally to “make the hand move” can intensify attention to the symptom. Moving with an external pulse, melody, accompaniment, or phrase may allow the body to act with less conscious interference.</p>
    <p>This is closely related to entrainment. In functional tremor, tapping a steady rhythm with the opposite hand may alter the tremor or draw it toward the tapping frequency. Therapeutically, external rhythm can also be used to retrain the frequency or intensity of movement.</p>
    <p>At the piano, this can happen naturally.</p>
    <p>The less affected hand establishes a stable pulse. The symptomatic hand is drawn into that pulse. The hands begin to synchronize. Each keystroke then produces an immediate sound.</p>
    <p>That immediate return of sound is particularly important.</p>
    <p>For the brain, sound is a clear consequence: “I moved this finger, and this sound occurred.” The causal relationship is immediate and perceptible.</p>
    <p>This may help rebuild the sense of agency.</p>
    <p>In FND, some patients describe the unsettling experience that their body does not feel fully under their own control. At the piano, a self-generated movement becomes audible in the external world. The player hears the result and adjusts the next movement.</p>
    <p>Motor command, bodily sensation, auditory feedback, and agency therefore become linked within one loop.</p>
    <p>Within that loop, the sensorimotor map can be relearned.</p>
    <p>The process can begin with something very simple: play one note with one hand, then alternate between two notes; establish a steady beat with the less affected hand; allow the symptomatic hand to join that beat; gradually coordinate both hands; and eventually shape a short phrase.</p>
    <p>This is more than piano practice. It provides repeated, safe experiences of a body that can move. Against the predictions “this hand will shake” or “this hand cannot move,” the person accumulates new sensory evidence: “this hand can produce sound,” “this hand can join a rhythm,” and “this hand can again be used as part of my body.”</p>
    <p>Through repetition, top-down predictions may gradually be revised, the sensorimotor map recalibrated, and the sense of agency strengthened.</p>
    <p>The piano also has another important dimension.</p>
    <p>Music can offer a safe space for self-expression.</p>
    <p>Experiences such as domestic violence can erode the ability to express one’s intentions, to feel safe within one’s own body, and to feel in control of the surrounding environment. The piano offers an opposite kind of experience.</p>
    <p>I play. I choose when the sound begins. I alter it through my own touch. I can express emotion as sound without first having to put it into words.</p>
    <p>This is more than motor training. It can support the recovery of the body, the recovery of agency, and the expression of experience in a safer form.</p>
    <p>Of course, this does not mean that piano playing alone cures FND. Good care begins with communicating the diagnosis clearly and affirming that the symptoms are real. Depending on the person, treatment may combine physiotherapy, occupational therapy, psychotherapy, and trauma-informed care. Education, eliciting preserved automatic movement, and retraining movement while attention is redirected are among the central principles.</p>
    <p>What this example shows is that music is not merely consolation.</p>
    <p>Music redirects attention. It provides rhythm. It synchronizes the body. It links movement with sensory feedback. It can support agency and create a space in which experiences that resist language can be expressed.</p>
    <p>Music may therefore provide a means of reorganizing the relationship between prediction in the brain and sensation from the body.</p>
    <p>Here, too, we encounter the theme of Human Experience.</p>
    <p>A small mismatch in neural prediction and a distortion in the map of the body can become the lived experience of tremor or weakness. Music may help translate that experience back into movement, sound, and agency.</p>
    <p>This is what I find so compelling at the intersection of music and medicine.</p>

    <h2>Sandplay Therapy and LEGO® SERIOUS PLAY®</h2>
    <p>Sandplay therapy is one form of psychotherapy used in psychiatry. Within a defined sandbox and a collection of objects, a person creates a scene freely in response to a theme. One advantage is that the subsequent conversation can focus on “the work” rather than directly on the self. The work is, in a sense, a projection of the creator and may reflect the person’s inner world. Yet discussing it at a small distance—as an object that can be observed and interpreted—can make it easier to reconsider one’s own experience and to comment on the work of others.</p>
    <p>LEGO® SERIOUS PLAY® follows a related direction outside medicine and is now used in many industries. The image on this slide comes from the LEGO® SERIOUS PLAY® website. Participants use LEGO bricks to construct models that reflect an idea or an aspect of their inner experience. Companies increasingly use this framework for generating ideas and gaining insight. Verbalized ideas are easier to grasp, but many of the images within us are vague, difficult to articulate, and not yet fully conscious. By making something first, explaining why it was made, reconsidering it, and rebuilding it, an image can gradually acquire form.</p>

    <h2>The Concept of Pointillism and the Concept of Research</h2>
    <p>This slide illustrates a method used by the head of my laboratory in a public lecture, drawing on material from the 2018 “Masters of Brain Science” course. His research sought to identify the body at the level of all of its individual cells. The presentation began with pointillism and emphasized its powerful underlying idea: something that appears continuous can be composed of innumerable small, discrete points. The same is true of a living organism. A large body is also an assembly of small units—individual cells. From there, the lecture posed a question: how can we observe every point while still understanding the whole? That question led naturally to the pioneering research program of tissue clearing and whole-body, single-cell analysis.</p>
    <p>Art also appears on the covers of scientific journals. Research concepts often become so complex that they are difficult to communicate to a broad audience. Yet every researcher wants to convey the core idea. Art can serve as the medium through which that central concept becomes intuitively accessible.</p>

    <h2>Vision</h2>
    <p>The title of this lecture is <em>From Brain Whisper to Human Experience</em>. For me, these words form an axis connecting medicine, research, music, work, and life.</p>
    <p>I want to keep listening for small changes through time, understanding them as human experience, and returning that understanding to society—whether through medicine, research, music, or other forms of work. I would like to close by describing how I hope to continue this practice and bring medicine and music together.</p>
    <p>As a physician, I want to see a patient not as a diagnostic label, but as a person changing through time.</p>
    <p>Disease, medication, daily life, sleep, emotion, and the social environment interact to create a person’s condition.</p>
    <p>Clinical practice requires us to hear not only the symptom visible now, but also the time through which the person has traveled to reach the present.</p>
    <p>I want to be a physician who sees the human being within time, rather than the diagnosis alone.</p>
    <p>As a researcher, I want to understand how small changes generate brain states.</p>
    <p>I want to study sleep, brain states, psychiatric disorders, and drug responses through mathematical models and data analysis.</p>

    <p>As a musician, I do not want to confine music to the category of a hobby.</p>

    <h2>Forming Your Own Question</h2>
    <p>Interdisciplinary fields will become increasingly important, and many of you may one day work at the intersection of music and something else. Yet interdisciplinary work is difficult. If approached superficially, it can end with a person sampling many areas without developing depth in any of them. In my own case, being a physician and a pianist as two separate identities may be enough for my well-being and personal enjoyment. But if the goal is to create new value, simply assembling an excellent physician and an excellent pianist would often be more effective. The question, then, is what someone who stands between disciplines can uniquely contribute.</p>
    <p>I believe the answer begins by pursuing what is essential in both fields and developing a question that is genuinely one’s own.</p>
    <p>In this lecture, I connected medicine and music by following change through time and by considering how interactions among small components create complex properties and dynamics at the level of the whole. I also described why the power of art is needed in medicine.</p>
    <p>When work becomes busy, our attention is easily consumed by implementation and minor adjustments. The underlying framework and the fundamental problem can be left behind. At such moments, it is important to pause and return to the questions: What is this essentially about? What problem do I truly want to solve?</p>
    <p>This is where standing in multiple fields can become a strength. Practicing this kind of inquiry in several disciplines can deepen our understanding of each one.</p>
    <p>Fields that appear completely different may share something fundamental while diverging in other important ways.</p>
    <p>When we are rooted in several fields, comparison allows us to see each of them more clearly.</p>
    <p>In my case, medicine and music both require careful interpretation and description of change through time. They differ, however, in their aims: medicine and science seek rigorous description through numbers and language, whereas art expresses aspects of experience that cannot always be contained by those symbols.</p>
    <p>We can therefore deepen the work of each field and, where necessary, bring into one field something that it lacks from another.</p>
    <p>I hope this offers one possible way to build an axis of your own.</p>
